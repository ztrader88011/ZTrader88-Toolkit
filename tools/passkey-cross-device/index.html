An authenticator app shows a fresh six-digit code every 30 seconds, which you type in after your password. The codes are made on your phone — they even work with no internet connection.

A passkey is different: it replaces the password itself. You sign in with your face, fingerprint, or PIN — the same way you unlock your device — and there's no code to type. Passkeys can't be copied or accidentally given away, which is what makes them resistant to phishing.

Our recommendation: passkey as your main lock — and keep an authenticator app set up as your spare key, so if you ever lose your passkey, the app still gets you back in.

Do

  • Use a passkey wherever one is offered — Google's own advice is to consider enrolling in passkeys.
  • Keep more than one way in, so a single lost phone can't lock you out.
  • Leave your phone's date and time set to automatic — authenticator codes depend on the correct time.

Don’t

  • Don’t read a code out to anyone who calls or texts asking for it — no real company will ever ask. That call is a break-in in progress.
  • Don’t type a code into a page you reached from an unexpected message: a typed code can be phished by a look-alike site; a passkey can't.

Three quick checks:

  1. Same account? Passkeys only appear on devices signed in to the same Apple Account or Google account that holds them.
  2. Bluetooth on, phone nearby? Google's own tip for the QR sign-in is: check that Bluetooth is on — the phone and the computer confirm they're side by side before anything works.
  3. Right door? On Google's sign-in, it's “Try another way,” then “Use your passkey.” On an Apple sign-in, look for “Passkey from nearby device.”

If it still won't work, use your backup — authenticator app or backup codes — rather than starting over. Google's “Try another way” exists for exactly that: it takes you back to your earlier sign-in choices.

Do

  • Work through the three checks above, in order.

Don’t
