A scammer takes a face people trust — a celebrity, a presenter, a familiar expert — and puts that face on an offer the person never made. The fake turns up as a sponsored ad, a random page, or a message, usually promising easy money or a miracle product. The link leads to a look-alike site, and the payment is asked for in ways that are hard or impossible to undo.

The person in the video usually knows nothing about it. Fake celebrity testimonials are banned outright under the FTC’s 2024 Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials — the fakes keep coming anyway, which is why the check has to happen on your side, before any money moves.

Because artificial intelligence (AI) can now build a convincing copy of a real face and voice — synthetic content that looks and sounds like the person, saying words they never said. Your eyes are no longer a reliable test, and that is exactly what the fakes count on.

The scale is not small. The Federal Bureau of Investigation’s (FBI) Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), in its 2025 Internet Crime Report, counted more than 22,000 complaints mentioning AI, with over $893 million lost. So do not judge by how real it looks — judge by the six signs in the test above: where it reached you, what it promises, and how it wants paying.

Act quickly, and stay calm — there are still real steps:

  1. Stop any further payments. Do not send “fees”, “taxes”, or “unlock” payments to get your money back — that is the same scam asking twice.
  2. Call your bank or the payment service and tell them it was a scam. Use the real number — the one on the back of your card, or on the company’s own website that you type in yourself. Never use a number or link from the ad.
  3. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Reporting is how these fakes get spotted and taken down for the next person.

In exactly two places, both reached by you — never through the ad:

  1. The person’s real account. Go to the account you already know is theirs. If they truly endorse it, it will be there too. If it is not there, treat the ad as fake.
  2. The company’s own website or app. Type the address in yourself, or open the app you already have. If the offer is real, the company will show it on its own front door.

Never check through the ad’s own link, button, or phone number — a fake ad points every one of those at the scammer, so “checking” there just asks the thief whether the theft is genuine.
