That Call From the "FTC" Is a Scam: How to Spot a Fake FTC Agent

The real Federal Trade Commission will never call you, threaten you, or ask you to move your money. Here is how the impersonators work — and the simple checks that stop them cold.

Plain-English online safety · ztrader88 · November 2026

The call sounds official. The person on the line says they are an agent with the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) — the U.S. government agency that protects consumers from fraud. They may read you a badge number. They may even use the name of a real FTC employee.

Then comes the story. A phone has been registered in your name, they say, and it is being used to run scams in another country. If you do not cooperate, they will disconnect your phone number. They offer to transfer you to a police officer so you can file a report. Later, the story gets worse: you are told you are under arrest for what "your" phone number did — unless you send money for a security deposit.

None of it is true. The FTC said so itself, in plain words, in a consumer alert about this exact call: it is all a scam. And the threat is the tell. Fear is the tool the whole scheme runs on — once you can see the tool, the scheme loses its power over you.

This is not a rare call

Last year, the FTC received more than 1 million reports about imposter scams — scams where a criminal pretends to be someone they are not — with reported losses of $3.5 billion. Many of those reports were about scammers pretending to be the FTC itself.

The impersonators do not stop at phone calls. They build fake FTC websites that copy the real one's name, logo, and design. They send texts, emails, and social media messages. They show people fake FTC credentials, including badges. Since the FTC's Impersonation Rule took effect in April 2024, the agency has demanded the removal of more than 30 fake FTC websites — and new ones keep appearing.

That is why the FTC published fresh guidance in November 2026 on how to spot the fakes. The good news is that the fakes all fail the same few checks.

Five things the real FTC will never do

This is the part worth remembering — and worth passing along to anyone who might get that call. A real FTC employee will never:

  • Ask you to send money — or connect you to anyone who tells you to send money. Not for a security deposit, not for a fine, not for anything.
  • Threaten to disconnect your phone. The FTC does not switch off phone numbers, and it does not threaten to.
  • Tell you to keep the conversation secret. Secrecy is the scammer's shield. A real government employee never needs you to hide a conversation from your family or your bank.
  • Text or email you a photo of an employee ID to "prove" who they are. A picture of a badge proves nothing — scammers make them up.
  • Tell you to move your money to "protect" it. Nobody legitimate will ever ask you to transfer money, hand over cash, buy gift cards, buy gold, or go to a Bitcoin ATM (a machine that converts cash into cryptocurrency). Every one of those requests is a scam, every time.

Read that list once more, because it works in both directions. If the person on the phone does any one of these five things, you do not need to wonder who they are. You know.

The website check that takes five seconds

The fake websites fail an even simpler test. Real FTC websites always end in .gov. The fakes use endings that look close — .com, .org, .us, .online — but close is not the same.

So before you type any personal information into a site that claims to be the FTC, look at the address. If it does not end in .gov, it is not the FTC. The FTC's real reporting site follows the same rule: ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

If the call comes, here is the whole drill

  • Hang up. You do not owe a stranger on the phone your time, your politeness, or your information. Hanging up is not rude — it is the correct move.
  • Do not use the contact information they give you. Scammers in this scheme have told people to search online for a police number and call it back — and the people who answer are part of the scam. If you want to check a story, find the number yourself, from a source you already trust.
  • Talk to someone you trust. A spouse, a child, a friend, your bank. Scammers push secrecy and speed precisely because a ten-minute conversation with someone who cares about you breaks the spell.
  • Report it. Tell the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Your report helps the agency track these schemes and take the fake sites down.

And if the call rattled you, that is understandable — it was designed to. It rattles people who have run businesses, courtrooms, and households. Being targeted says nothing about you. What you do next is the part that is yours.

Pass it on

The people most likely to get this call are the people least likely to go looking for an article like this one. So if there is someone in your family who would stay on the line out of politeness, or out of worry, send this to them. The five "never" checks above fit on an index card. They are worth a place by the phone.
